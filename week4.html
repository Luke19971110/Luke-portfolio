Weekly Reflections

Week 4

Building a Dataset

During this Thursday's workshop, our task was to build a dataset through group work. We chose Scenario One, in which we acted as members of a Student Advisory Committee investigating how universities in the UK can understand how students use generative AI in their studies. Following the six steps introduced by the lecturer in class, we began imagining and generating data. First, we narrowed our research question down to how students use AI when it becomes involved in humanities learning and essay writing, and what difficulties they may encounter during this process. Based on this question, we adopted five variables, each with five values, in order to classify and quantify the data.

When considering data sources, we chose to collect data from posts on the Reddit community r/UniUK. We searched for relevant posts by alternately using combinations of hashtags related to specific AI tools and keywords such as humanities and essay. After collecting the data, we integrated it into a spreadsheet. We first carried out data cleaning and removed posts unrelated to our research topic. Then, according to the content of the posts, we classified the students' posts based on the five variables and their corresponding values that we had previously defined, producing specific numerical results. In terms of research ethics, we ensured that metadata would not be published. We anonymised user information within the dataset and summarised or paraphrased users' posts to ensure that their identities could not be traced, preventing any unexpected harm to users.

Although it seemed that we had successfully collected a small dataset, quantified it into numerical values, and carried out ethically appropriate treatment, new questions and concerns emerged during the practical process.

1. Concerning Our Data Source

We chose the r/UniUK community because we found that most active users there are students studying at UK universities. Within our variable "Discipline Subfield", we divided students into five disciplinary categories. However, very few students explicitly stated which department or degree programme they belonged to in their posts. As a result, many students were classified into category 5: Interdisciplinary / Unspecified. This suggests that this community may not be an appropriate data source. Perhaps we should find more specialised communities for data collection. For example, finding subreddit communities specifically for arts and humanities students might better match our research subjects.

2. Concerning Our Method of Classification

This issue became apparent when we reflected on the variable Trust Level. We designed three levels of trust in AI: Low Trust, Moderate Trust (Provisional), and High Trust. Since students' posts are textual, they rarely explicitly state their level of trust in AI. As a result, determining which category a post belongs to became a very subtle issue. For example, students who clearly stated that they used AI for cheating or writing essays on their behalf were classified as having high trust in AI, while students who explicitly stated that they would not use AI were classified as low trust. However, what about Moderate Trust (Provisional)? This is highly debatable. What standards should we use to judge it?

In reality, even high trust and low trust are not entirely absolute. For example, some students enthusiastically shared that they had used AI to create a program to help manage their study time and published it online. At first glance, this seems to demonstrate a high level of trust in AI's capabilities. However, they later stated that the program might contain bugs and would need further testing and repair. Does this represent a middle level of trust in AI? Furthermore, even students who use AI for cheating or essay writing may not actually trust AI very much. They may simply wish to avoid the effort required for studying or place their hopes on not being caught committing academic misconduct. Even without AI, they might seek other methods to achieve the same outcome. Should this really be blamed on AI? Similarly, many people within the low-trust category explicitly argued that AI should not be directly used in essay writing, yet at the same time strongly trusted AI's ability to help with grammar correction. Is this not also a certain degree of high trust?

Clearly, the variable concerning trust in AI appears too ambiguous, and the standards used to determine values rely heavily on our subjective judgement. This seems likely to produce misclassification and bias, which is obviously problematic. Thinking further about this issue, within platform capitalism, are the data extracted from us also being incorrectly classified through similarly ambiguous variables? Are the people who classify these data, much like ourselves, constructing a framework of inequality based on subjective judgement and bias? I began to think that perhaps the issue is not simply whether data classification itself is accurate, but rather how classification systems themselves shape the reality that we imagine and expect to see.

3. Observations from Social Media Data

By observing the social media data collected from r/UniUK, we noticed that students' posts seemed to represent two relatively extreme emotional positions. One group shared their achievements in using AI for learning with a very optimistic and positive attitude. The other group expressed strong anxiety and frustration, for example concerns about academic misconduct caused by the use of AI in studying and essay writing. Very few students calmly shared their experiences of using AI in learning. This led us to reconsider whether collecting data about students and AI through social media might overlook a large number of invisible students. Perhaps these students neither eve especially exciting outcomes when using AI, and therefore do not feel motivated to post about it.

Future Improvements

If we had sufficient time to carefully redesign our dataset creation process, I think we would approach the potential binary oppositions within our variables more cautiously. We would also adopt multiple approaches to data collection from different perspectives, such as focus group interviews, offline surveys, participatory activities about AI within student communities, and the use of other open public datasets. Collecting and comparing data through these methods alongside social media data collection may help address some of the problems that currently exist in our quantitative research.
