Weekly Reflections

Week 2

This week, we learned HTML and CSS, as well as how to deploy our own websites online through a CMS and GitHub. Although this was not my first encounter with these technologies, I am clearly not a professional web designer, and revisiting them still felt rather difficult.

As for coding software, I had never used Visual Studio Code before. Compared to Atom, which I briefly experimented with in 2021, the feature that immediately caught my attention was the Copilot panel on the right-hand side of the interface. Through it, I could ask Copilot about the meaning of code, have it modify templates, and even help perform various coding tasks. It felt almost magical.

Thinking back to last year's discussions around the concept of Vibe Coding, I began to realise that what once sounded like a fantasy may already have become reality. I decided to explore it for myself, and with Copilot's help I created a webpage displaying lyrics from Lorde songs (a good opportunity to recommend one of my favourite artists). The result genuinely surprised me.

While reading the code Copilot had produced, I found myself wondering: if I cannot fully understand the code, how am I supposed to maintain the website?

Of course, this is a relatively simple website in both structure and content. But what if it were a large-scale project built entirely by AI? What happens when a bug appears that it cannot fix on its own? If the underlying code and algorithms contain unfair or discriminatory assumptions, from what perspective should I audit them? Is Vibe Coding creating a new and dynamic technological black box between algorithms and natural language, only packaged in a more approachable and enjoyable way?

What if natural language itself is unable to describe the effect I have in mind? What if I provide a highly controversial instruction? Would that "teach" it something harmful, such as content involving ethical concerns, or even writing a hacking program to attack another website? Is AI playing the role of a Stand?

(The concept of a Stand comes from the Japanese animated series JoJo's Bizarre Adventure. In that world, both heroes and villains possess a Stand. These Stands obey their users while also being capable of acting independently. They can be used for good or ill, and as their users develop a deeper understanding of their abilities, their Stand's special powers often become stronger, sometimes even evolving into entirely new forms. I see some similarities here with the automation of algorithms and their capacity for dynamic adaptation and evolution. A comparable analogy might be Pokémon, but that framework feels a little too romanticised for what I am trying to describe here, so I would rather not invoke it.)

Perhaps I have wandered too far from the topic. Let me return to this week's reading.

Gillespie, T. (2013). The Relevance of Algorithms. In: Gillespie, T., Boczkowski, P. J., and Foot, K. A. (eds.), Media Technologies: Essays on Communication, Materiality, and Society. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, pp. 167–193.

This article is difficult to understand. I am still reading and revisiting it, and it continues to generate many questions. What particularly interests me is the author's rhetoric.

In the article, she writes:

1. "Raw data is an oxymoron."
Why is this the case? And how does the concept of metadata differ from Raw data or data itself?

2. "Data is both already desiccated and persistently messy."
What kinds of conditions are being described by the terms desiccated and persistently messy? What implications does this state create?

3. She argues that, in order for algorithms to function automatically, information must first be rendered into data.
How should this statement be understood?

Throughout the text, she distinguishes between the concepts of information and data. What does each term represent? This also prompted a broader question for me: why do we speak of digital media rather than information media? The rhetorical choice is fascinating, because different terms often signal very different contexts. For example, data mining and data extraction convey rather different assumptions and implications, as discussed in the Week 3 reading for COMM5785.


This week, I attended a special concertThe World of Hans Zimmer – A New Dimension and had an amazing experience. During the Interstellar suite, the low-frequency vibrations seemed to travel straight through my body. It felt almost like receiving a full-body massage. It was a remarkable experience.

Before going to the concert, I also visited Noodle House, currently my favourite Chinese restaurant in Leeds. Their Sichuan Boiled Beef (which sounds surprisingly light and healthy, but is actually a classic Sichuan dish that is both intensely spicy and packed with flavour) was absolutely delicious. Taken together, these experiences gave me more than enough energy to continue doing the things that are within my reach.


Finally, I owe an apology, both to myself and to the readers of this website. Tuesday's reflection was not updated until Wednesday. Real life has simply been too busy, leaving me only brief moments to organise what currently feels like a tangled digital space.

Calling it a digital space rather than a data space, a weekly reflections column, or even a portfolio website (if it can truly be called one) is an interesting question in itself, and one that is also tied to rhetoric.

For me, this is a dynamic space for relaxation and enjoyment. It is a place where I can freely express myself and leave traces of my thoughts and experiences while studying in Leeds.

So for now, let Luke continue talking to himself, doodling, collecting fragments, and piecing them together within this little corner of the digital world.
