Weekly Reflections

Week 8

Allergy Experience Map

This allergy experience map records my experience of having an allergic reaction in the Roger Stevens Building. I am allergic to humidity, pollen, and some other unknown substances that I may come into contact with unintentionally. My main allergy symptoms include allergic dermatitis, red and swollen eyes, and difficulty breathing.

Having lived with allergies for many years, I have become highly sensitive to sensations of heat, swelling, and itching on the surface of my skin, while also being able to selectively ignore them. When I try to engage senses other than sight, itching becomes a new way through which I perceive the world. It emerges through my interactions and contact with all things.

Because the humidity was particularly high that day, my allergic reactions were generally very severe, making it difficult for me to distinguish which specific area of the building caused the mildest allergic response. However, what can be clearly observed is that the reddest area shown on the map, where I scratched my skin most intensely, corresponds to the toilet on the eighth floor of the Roger Stevens Building. My skin felt extremely uncomfortable and itchy there. I also experienced particularly intense itching in the garden outside the building because of the rain and the surrounding plants. In addition, it is worth noting that my allergy symptoms are also influenced by my psychological and cognitive state. For example, seeing flowers can make me feel itchy even when they do not produce pollen, and seeing damp environments in television programmes can also trigger an itching sensation.

As a form of counter-mapping practice, I attempt to create a map of my own allergic experience using colours sampled from the affected and unaffected areas of my skin. I use scratching and smearing to represent the interactions between the environment and the surface of my skin.

This work follows the counter-mapping methodology of WalkingLab (https://walkinglab.org/podcast/walking-as-counter-mapping/). Through embodied experience, I sense my entanglement with the environment. It abandons the colonial tendency of conventional cartography to use mapping to demarcate resources and appropriate land, and instead turns towards a discussion of the relationships between myself and all beings and things in the natural world, in order to explore a more-than-human form of mapping.

Allergy, as a symptom that emerges when I encounter plants, moisture, air, temperature, and animals, is also a mode of perception and functions as a mapping practice that moves beyond anthropocentrism. I believe that moving beyond the human centre does not mean being anti-human. Rather, it produces a new relational subjectivity that emerges from paying attention to the entanglement between humans and things in the world. Here, this relational subjectivity is represented by allergic symptoms.

Conventional cartography places precision and “objectivity” above all else. In addition to its colonial tendency to appropriate land, race, and culture as resources, it also manifests a form of classificatory hegemony. By establishing standards and categories, this classificatory power gives value to its own perspective while silencing other perspectives. This ethical choice is dangerous (Searle et al., 2024).

In my personal experience, conventional cartography overlooks individual embodied experience. I once lived in Nanjing, China, a beautiful city with a rich cultural and historical heritage. However, I found it extremely difficult to move through the city because plane trees and willow trees were planted throughout it. During spring and summer, when I used smartphone maps to navigate the city’s streets towards my destination, I was often suddenly attacked by pollen and willow catkins. However, wearing a face mask and protective goggles made breathing very uncomfortable. Therefore, last year, I temporarily said goodbye to the city in which I had lived for more than eight years. Understanding a city often begins with a map, but leaving a city may involve far more complex reasons.
