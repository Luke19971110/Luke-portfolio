Weekly Reflections

WEEK 1

This week marked the official start of the MA Digital Media programme and my third week in Leeds.

To briefly summarise my first impressions, Leeds is a small yet beautiful city. It combines a rich historical heritage with modern shopping centres and cinemas, and, most importantly, an incredible variety of delicious food. What makes it even more appealing is that everything is within around a twenty-minute walk, making the city both convenient and comfortable to live in.

Our first lecture was led by Holly. She is humorous, approachable, and engaging. When explaining course concepts, she frequently uses vivid examples that make complex ideas much easier to understand. Before and after class, she was always willing to answer questions and offer support, which created a welcoming and encouraging learning environment.

Notes and Reflections
This Week's Reading
Baym, N. K. (2010). Chapter 2: Making New Media Make Sense. In Personal Connections in the Digital Age (pp. 30–53). Cambridge: Polity.

Four Perspectives on Technology and Society

Technological Determinism
• When technological innovations emerge, people often find it difficult to resist their influence, particularly during the early stages of adoption.
Social Construction of Technology
• Human actors are the primary drivers of both technological development and social change.
Social Shaping
• The relationship between technology and society is reciprocal; both continuously influence one another.
Domestication
• Through everyday use and integration, technologies become familiar and eventually taken for granted.

Key Questions Raised by the Author

Baym explores how societies move from viewing new technologies as either threatening or exciting to accepting them as ordinary, almost invisible parts of everyday life.
She argues that understanding the social impact of digital media requires attention not only to the characteristics of technologies themselves but also to the social practices, discourses, and forms of rhetoric that emerge around them and shape their development.
Importantly, while the perspectives of social shaping and domestication reveal the complexity of the relationship between technology and society, this complexity should not lead us to abandon the search for insights. Instead, we should remain cautious of overly simple explanations.

Benjamin, R. (2019). Engineered Inequity: Are Robots Racist? In Race After Technology. Cambridge: Polity Press.

I found the examples discussed in this chapter particularly striking, including AI beauty contests, the relationship between robotics and slavery, automatic soap dispensers, and predictive policing algorithms.

Benjamin argues that algorithms are not neutral. Instead, they often contain embedded biases and inequalities that may reinforce existing social stereotypes and structures of discrimination. She encourages readers to be sceptical of the optimistic promises frequently associated with new technologies and to ask what interests and assumptions lie beneath these narratives.

The author further argues that unintended harm should not be treated as an excuse for bias or injustice. Technology companies that benefit from these systems should also be held accountable for their consequences.

However, some of the discussion surrounding China's social credit system did not fully align with my own experiences of living in China. This has prompted me to conduct further reading and research before forming a more definitive judgement on the topic.

Reflection

For me, this week was primarily about discovering pathways into the study of digital media: identifying the perspectives, concepts, and methods through which we can understand, interpret, and critically analyse the digital technologies that permeate everyday life.

The readings encouraged me to think about what digital media researchers should pay attention to, what forms of critique are possible, and what kinds of social and political questions these technologies raise. These reflections have been intellectually energising and have given me greater anticipation for the modules ahead.
