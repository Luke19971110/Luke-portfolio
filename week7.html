Weekly Reflections

Week 7

Data and Embodied Experience

This week I watched We Were Here, a brilliant short film. The film tells a story of resistance to AI. By drawing a shaky line, it speaks to embodied experience in life and, from private, familial, and social perspectives, critiques what AI omits in data extraction, data classification, and result prediction, what it cannot understand, and the dangerous classification power that exclude whom. In addition, the film offers a way to resist AI: data interference. By feeding it incorrect information, the film shows AI misidentifying a pizza as a completely different food. Through the emergence of glitches, the short film reveals AI’s speculative character rather than its existence as a myth of infallible technology. The shaky line, as an outward manifestation of embodied experience, responds to the cognitive shortcomings of automated programs: their lack of interactive knowledge of the world.

From early history of data, the embodied experiences that are overlooked are often as important as what the data appears to show. In The Atlas of AI: Power, Politics, and the Planetary Costs of Artificial Intelligence, Crawford points out that the female labor that historically built data and ensured systems ran was overlooked, while only the engineers who designed computers during wartime are remembered, even though both were equally important. (Crawford, 2021) Receiving information, organizing it, and punching holes—these embodied forms of labor in data processing are overlooked. Without consulting history, few people know how important “that room full of girls” were to today’s computing and algorithmic systems.

The reason embodied experience is overlooked is that data has been packaged by neoliberalism as a harmless resource. Crawford points out that the phrase “data is oil” turns data into an abstract, immaterial state, concealing its origins and purposes of use, thereby rendering care, consent, and accountability ineffective. (Crawford, 2021) This phenomenon appears not only in the extraction of data but also frequently in classification and power structure construction. In Data Feminism, D’Ignazio and Klein (2020) argue that data must be classified before it can be used, and point out that the absurd classification methods of past scientific racism are returning in the present. Questioning the methodology of classification is as important as discussing the power of classification. Cheney-Lippold points out that algorithmic identities constructed through classification stand in an epistemological and ontological division from people’s own identity characteristics. Corruption, as a feature of algorithmic identity, replaces the reflexive interaction in the process of social construction. (Cheney-Lippold, 2017) On this basis, the extraction and classification of data, under the banner of a mythologized neutral science and infused with the ideologies of accelerationism, neoliberalism, and colonialism, continue to advance. In his satirical song about the American technology industry, Tom Lehrer mentions that:

“Once the rockets are up, who cares where they come down?”
“That’s not my department.” Says Wernher Von Braun.

So, which department is responsible for the embodied experiences we have lost?

However, I do not fully agree with the protagonists’ resistance to algorithms in the film. Even when such resistance is romanticized into a Quixotic form, we still need to discuss the concrete impact of automated programs in complex living environments. The various critiques of the inequalities and shortcomings of automated programs described above remain highly important and should not be dismissed as technophobia. As D’Ignazio and Klein argue, by addressing the methodological question of who is counted and for whom they are counted, counting can also become a force for healing. (D'Ignazio and Klein, 2020) By asking who collects the data behind automated programs, for whom it is classified, and what purpose it serves, these programs may, under the standards of ethics and fairness, also have the potential to empower communities from inside and outside. Only if the inside of the black box is made transparent and fair can the possibility of “data communism” be realized; the ghost Marx called for may become the key to opening the black-box programs.
