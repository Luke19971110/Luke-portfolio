Weekly Reflections

Week 5

Data Visualisation

Data visualisation is the theme of this week, but I feel that it is integrated with the creation of datasets from last week and these two data-related processes are difficult to separate. Clearly, data visualisation has its own set of creative conventions, different from those of filmmaking or photographic art, but to some extent, they all contain similar traces and clues in their historical and cultural backgrounds.

This feeling reminds me of similar situations I encountered in my previous work experience. When working on a photography or video project, I would plan the script in advance, shoot according to the script, and then edit and produce the material, constructing what could be called a montage. To me, data collection is like preparing a script and shooting footage and images. It requires us first to imagine the data that needs to be collected, establish a working standard, and then begin the collection process.

Data visualisation is like the process of editing and creating visual effects. Existing materials are interpreted and arranged from the director's perspective before finally being presented to the audience. Clearly, just like any documentary project or photographic work that claims to be “objective”, data visualisation always contains subjective editing and interpretation.


After reading Feigenbaum, “Visual Data Storytelling”, I gained a preliminary understanding of data visualisation. I really liked some of the ideas proposed by the authors, which I found very inspiring.

For example, when creating a data visualisation, we should not use a single view from nowhere. Instead, we should plan how the visualisation is expressed with the aim of reaching multiple truths.

This point moved me deeply. When collecting and visualising data, we should not regard variables and datasets merely as numbers. They are all real human beings. It is really important to listen to different kinds of voices and to think more about ways to make everyone happy without causing harm.

I have always held stereotypes about numbers and statistics. I really dislike using numbers and simple labels to represent a person (for example, for a period of time, it was very popular for people to display their MBTI personality labels on social media, because I think that, in an era when people increasingly pursue self-fulfilment and the awakening of self-awareness, simplifying and flattening themselves for the sake of efficiency in making friends is a very contradictory thing...).

However, through my recent studies, I have realised that this understanding is one-sided. Collecting data and visualising it as images can also examine existing power structures and promote empowerment. It can also support healing within communities and provide support and energy to people who are confused, lost, oppressed or forgotten. When facing the cold reality of capitalist realism, it can provide some evidence that we exist and that we are represented.


In addition to considering the meaning of visualisation, semiotic theories concerning the narrative role of data visualisation also support our understanding of how a good work should be presented visually. Stuart Hall's encoding and decoding theory tells us to ask: who is the author of the visualisation, and who is the audience?

Context, standpoint and perspective all affect the process through which the work is encoded and decoded by the audience. Symbols, colours, captions and editorial choices all matter. There is a great deal to learn about data visualisation, and it is necessary to continue summarising and reflecting through practice.

This is the data visualisation assignment that our group completed in class. Did it meet these requirements? Clearly not. So, I should continue thinking hard about these questions in my work.


Some thoughts about life:

This week has been so busy. Seems there are endless tasks pushing me forwards (I have realised that, much of the time, my busyness is caused by my own stubbornness and is something I have brought upon myself, and perhaps it is a meaningless struggle).

As a result, I once again did not update my Week 5 reflection until Week 6. Time management is genuinely a major problem for me. Simply completing my academic tasks and reaching a standard that satisfies me is already difficult, and I have not even properly enjoyed the pleasures of life.

It was only after my final class on Friday that I realised that tonight was already the final night of Leeds Light Night. Perhaps this would have been a wonderful opportunity to develop a deeper emotional connection with the city, but I missed it because I had to meet an approaching deadline.

When I passed a church-like building in Woodhouse, I heard sounds coming from inside a light installation. They sounded like fluctuations in cosmic frequencies, as though they were transmitting some kind of signal to me.

The emotion I felt at that moment is genuinely difficult to describe. Perhaps it was a mixture of vulnerability and determination? Many emotions that words could not describe became entangled inside my body. How should I share them with you?
